\section{Comparator grid and timing boundaries}

In the revised two-dimensional t-SNE protocol, \pkg{fastEmbedR} reports its
selected grid in each fit. The pinned FIt-SNE wrapper exposes interpolation
points, minimum intervals, and intervals per coordinate unit. The benchmark
requests three interpolation points and minimum interval counts of 43 below
40,000 rows and 86 otherwise. FIt-SNE rounds these to supported box counts
of at least 50 and 90, respectively, corresponding to at least 150 and 270
interpolation nodes per axis. The realized grid can be larger if the current
coordinate span requires it; the wrapper does not return that size. These
settings approximate rather than exactly match the \pkg{fastEmbedR} CPU
128- and 256-cell policies. FIt-SNE three-dimensional Barnes--Hut runs do
not use the interpolation grid.

The tested cuML 26.6.0 implementation fixes three interpolation points and
50 intervals per axis in its two-dimensional FFT source, yielding a
300-cell padded transform axis. Its public Python t-SNE constructor does not
expose a grid-resolution parameter, so a matched-grid cuML comparison would
require modifying and rebuilding cuML. The benchmark records this fixed
source policy, but does not mislabel the runs as grid matched. UMAP methods
do not use this t-SNE FFT grid.

The September 30 workflow campaign has five same-seed timing repetitions
after warm-up for timing-eligible methods; jobs reaching the long-run
budget may have one completed fit. All input matrices were fitted in full.
The 7,200-second per-method limit and failed campaign audit are reflected
in Table~\ref{tab:all-method-performance}. The embedding gallery below
comes from a separate, capped September 29 campaign and must not be
read as visual evidence for the September 30 timings.

\section{Approximate-neighbor calibration}

The reported CPU HNSW workflows used a fixed, shape- and $k$-based policy,
not a search over parameters fitted to each evaluation data set. The
requested 0.99 recall is therefore an objective rather than a measured
guarantee. Exact-reference queries in the campaign were used for auditing
after settings had been chosen; their cost is diagnostic, not a hidden
component of the fixed-policy fit time. A result below 0.99 remains a
recall miss regardless of its runtime.

A data-adaptive comparison requires a separate arm. Candidate index-build
settings ($M$ and \code{efConstruction}) must be distinguished from the
runtime search setting \code{efSearch}. Candidate selection may use exact
neighbors of a seeded tuning-query sample, but not the disjoint holdout
queries used to report final recall. The selected settings and their
selection rule must be frozen before the holdout and downstream UMAP or
t-SNE quality evaluations. End-to-end adaptive time includes exact-reference
generation, candidate construction and search, and the final index and
embedding fit. Search-only time and projections amortized over repeated
queries are reported separately, never substituted for that total. The
calibration budget, query IDs, source identity, build/search parameters,
thread count, and observed recall distribution must accompany each result.
An untouched query set checks selection within a data set, whereas transfer
to new data sets checks whether the policy itself generalizes. A data set
used to design the candidate grid is not an independent test of that grid.
No adaptive-policy speed or recall result is inferred from the fixed-policy
campaign in this supplement.

\section{Experimental Deep1B file-backed study}

The current design uses one source-locked row window of the Deep1B
96-variable float32 input. The first fitted row is source row 1,000,001;
the 1- and 10-million-row cases use nested prefixes of that window.
This is a test of real-data execution and scaling, not an independent
cross-data-set generalization experiment. Table~\ref{tab:file-backed-design}
lists requested jobs, not completed observations. The ordinary 1-million-row
fit and the file-backed 1-million-row fit are the only matched-input route
comparison. An explicit full-row graph optimizer receives every row; it is
not equivalent to landmark fitting. The candidate graph can still use
approximate HNSW or IVF neighbors.

Execution audit, search quality, and hardware-use audit are distinct.
The file-backed CPU study records effective stage-level core counts;
the present CPU UMAP, Leiden, and Louvain optimizer calls request one
worker even in four-, eight-, or 16-core allocations. Their allocation
comparison is not an optimizer strong-scaling experiment.
Device-wide memory samples must name
each allocated GPU and distinguish its baseline from its peak. Two-GPU
PCA must distinguish distributed fitting from score projection. The
coordinated multi-GPU optimizers require per-device work and quality
checks; allocation alone is not proof of acceleration. An incomplete
multi-device memory trace is a measurement

otherwise failed job remains diagnostic.

The measured KNN stage includes its exact-reference audit. Build, query,
and audit times are stored separately; its combined stage time must not be
compared with a search-only comparator. The CPU HNSW record must include
effective per-shard $M$, \code{efConstruction}, and \code{efSearch}.
The fixed 128-query sample is not an untouched tuning holdout. A 0.99
recall claim requires its stated statistic and a separate holdout check;
an execution \texttt{PASS} with a recall warning is not a quality pass.

\subsection{Checkpoint and restart audit}

The experimental full-graph kNN builders commit completed reference
shards to a checkpoint sidecar. To test recovery independently of layout
optimization, we generated the same 8,000-row, 16-variable float32
input for CPU HNSW and one-device CUDA IVF, with $k=5$, four 2,000-row
reference shards, 1,000-row query blocks, and seed 19. Each arm built a
fresh checkpointed graph, killed a second process after a committed
shard, and restarted it from the same partial files. The audit required
the restart log to begin after the last committed shard, identical
effective search settings, and byte-identical final neighbor-ID and
float32-distance files. Exact-reference recall was sampled on 32 query
rows. The 512-MB control constrained algorithm buffers, not process RSS.

\section{Graph-supported layout-guided cluster merging}

The optional \code{layout\_merge} control in \code{graph\_cluster()} is an
exploratory postprocessing rule for a resident graph, not a replacement for
Leiden, Louvain, or Walktrap. PAGA motivates assessing connectivity between
partitions on the original neighbor graph rather than inferring it from the
distance between two-dimensional clusters \citep{wolf2019paga}. For each
pair of communities, our rule divides the number of positive-weight
cross-edges by its expected count under random assignment of graph edge
ends. It also divides the median plotted length of those cross-edges by
the median plotted length of all within-community graph edges. A pair is
eligible only when it has at least two cross-edges, connectivity at least
0.2, and length ratio at most 2. Eligible pairs are joined by connected
components. These thresholds are explicit defaults, not a calibrated
biological criterion; the rule and its layout condition are our adaptation,
\emph{not} the published PAGA estimator or a claim that visual proximity
establishes a shared cell state. t-SNE geometry in particular depends on
its visualization settings \citep{kobak2019}.

Figure~\ref{fig:layout-merge-synthetic} is a controlled 18-vertex synthetic
example generated by \code{analysis/layout\_merge\_showcase.R}. The two
adjacent six-vertex communities have three original-graph links and meet
both gates (connectivity 0.258; length ratio 1.00); the third also has
three links but fails the layout gate (ratio 28.78). The result contains
the original and merged memberships, both modularity values, resolved
thresholds, and a pair-level decision table. This is a mechanism
demonstration, not evidence that clustering accuracy improves on the
benchmark data sets; such a claim needs labels or independent structure
and sensitivity analysis across UMAP/t-SNE seeds.
